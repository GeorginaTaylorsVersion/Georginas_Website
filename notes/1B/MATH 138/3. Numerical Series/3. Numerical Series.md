# 3. Numerical Series

### 3.1 Introduction to Series

**3.1.1 Infinite Series**

Let $\{a_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ be a sequence of real numbers. An *infinite series* is a symbolic expression of the form 

$a_1 + a_2 + a_3 + a_4 + \cdots = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n.$

**3.1.3 Partial Sum**

Given an infinite series $\displaystyle \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$, we define its sequence of partial sums, $\{S_m\}_{m=1}^{\infty}$, as  

$S_m \;=\; a_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_m \;=\; \sum_{n=1}^m a_n$. 

$S_m$ is called the $m$-th partial sum of the infinite series.

Note that $S_m$ is the sum of the initial terms in the sequence $\{a_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ up to (and including) $a_m$.

**3.1.5 Convergence and Divergence**

We say that an infinite series $\displaystyle \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ converges to $S \in \mathbb{R}$ if $\lim_{m \to \infty} S_m \;=\; \lim_{m \to \infty} \left(\sum_{n=1}^m a_n\right) \;=\; S$. 

Here, $S$ is called the sum of the infinite series, and we write $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n \;=\; S$. 

If $\{S_m\}{m=1}^{\infty}$ diverges, then we say that $\displaystyle \sum{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ diverges. 

If $\lim_{m \to \infty} S_m \;=\; \pm\infty$, then we say that $\displaystyle \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$diverges to $\pm\infty$.

e.g. Telescoping series

Consider the infinite series ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \left(\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n+1}\right)}$. 

Its first three partial sums are:

$\begin{aligned} & S_{1}=1-\frac{1}{2}, \\[5pt]& S_{2}=\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{3}\right)=1-\frac{1}{3}, \\[5pt]& S_{3}=\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{3}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{3}-\frac{1}{4}\right)=1-\frac{1}{4}. \end{aligned}$

Using mathematical induction, we can show that ${\displaystyle S_{m}=1-\frac{1}{m+1}}$. 

Thus we have ${\displaystyle \lim _{m \rightarrow \infty } S_{m}=\lim _{m \rightarrow \infty } \left(1-\frac{1}{m+1} \right)=1-0=1}$. It follows that our series converges to 1.

**3.1.10 Geometric Series**

Let $a, x \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $a \neq 0$. A geometric series is a series of the form 

$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a x^n = a + ax + ax^2 + ax^3 + \cdots$. 

Here, $a$ is called the first term of the geometric series, and $x$ is called the ratio of the geometric series. We have chosen the convention that for a geometric series, the index $n$ starts at $n = 0$ instead of the usual $n = 1$.

We define the $m$-th partial sum of the geometric series to be 

$S_m = \sum_{n=0}^m a x^n = a + ax + ax^2 + \cdots + ax^m.$

Note that $S_m$ for a geometric series contains $m + 1$ terms instead of the usual $m$ terms. 

We have chosen the convention that the index $m$ in $S_m$ indicates that the sum stops at the $n = m$ term (and does *not* indicate that the number of terms in $S_m$ is $m$).

**3.1.11 Geometric Series Test**

Let $\displaystyle \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a x^n$ be a geometric series, where $a \neq 0$.

1. If $\lvert x \rvert < 1$, then $\displaystyle \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a x^n$ converges and $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a x^n = \frac{a}{1 - x}$
2. If $\lvert x \rvert \ge 1$, then $\displaystyle \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a x^n$ diverges.

e.g. 

1. ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=0}^{\infty }\frac{3}{10}\left(\frac{1}{10}\right)^{n} =\frac{3}{10}\sum _{n=0}^{\infty }\left(\frac{1}{10}\right)^{n} =\frac{3}{10}\left(1+\frac{1}{10}+\frac{1}{100}+\cdots \right) =\frac{3}{10}+\frac{3}{100}+\frac{3}{1000}+\cdots }$is a geometric series with $a=\dfrac {3}{10}$ and ${\displaystyle x=\frac{1}{10}}$. It converges to ${\displaystyle \frac{a}{1-x}=\frac{\frac{3}{10}}{1-\frac{1}{10}}=\frac{{3}/{10}}{{9}/{10}}=\frac{3}{10}\cdot \frac{10}{9} =\frac{1}{3}}$ since ${\displaystyle |x|=\left|\frac{1}{10}\right|=\frac{1}{10} \lt 1}$. 
2. ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=0}^{\infty } 5\left(-\frac{3}{2}\right)^{n}}$is a geometric series with $a=5$ and ${\displaystyle x=-\frac{3}{2}}$. It diverges since ${\displaystyle |x|=\left|-\frac{3}{2}\right|=\frac{3}{2}\geq 1}$. 

### 3.2 Arithmetic Properties of Series and the Divergence Test

**3.2.1 Linearity of Infinite Series**

Suppose that $\displaystyle \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n = A$ and $\displaystyle \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n = B$, where $A, B \in \mathbb{R}$.

1. For any $c \in \mathbb{R}$, $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c\,a_n = cA$
2. $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (a_n \pm b_n) = A \pm B$

**3.2.2 Tail**

Given an infinite series $(\displaystyle \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$, let $S_m \;=\; a_1 \;+\; a_2 \;+\; \cdots \;+\; a_m$ denote its $m$-th partial sum. 

The difference $\Bigl(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n\Bigr) \;-\; S_m
\;=\; a_{m+1} \;+\; a_{m+2} \;+\; a_{m+3} \;+\; \cdots
\;=\; \sum_{n=m+1}^{\infty} a_n$ 

is called a tail of the infinite series $\displaystyle \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$.

**3.2.3 Tail Convergence Theorem**

Let $\{a_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ be a sequence and let $j$ denote a positive integer.

1. If $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ converges, then its tail $\sum_{n=j}^{\infty} a_n$ also converges for each $j \ge 1$.
2. If the tail $\sum_{n=j}^{\infty} a_n$ converges for some $j$, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ also converges.

**3.2.4 Tail Divergence Theorem**

Let $j$ denote a positive integer. 

1. If a tail $\sum_{n=j}^{\infty} a_n$ diverges for some $j$, then the original series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ diverges too.
2. If $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$$ diverges, then its tail $\sum_{n=j}^{\infty} a_n$ diverges for each $j \ge 1$.

**3.2.5 Necessary Condition for Convergence**

If $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ converges, then $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = 0$.

**3.2.6 Divergence Test**

If $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n \neq 0$, then $\displaystyle \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ diverges.

### 3.3 Integral Test

**3.3.1 Positive Series**

An infinite series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ is said to be positive (or nonnegative) if $a_n \ge 0$ for all $n \ge 1$.
It is said to be strictly positive if $a_n > 0$ for all $n \ge 1$.

It is said to be eventually positive (or eventually nonnegative) if there is a positive integer $j$ such that $a_n \ge 0$ for all $n \ge j$.

Finally, it is said to be eventually strictly positive if there is a positive integer $j$ such that $a_n > 0$ for all $n \ge j$.

**3.3.2** 

Let $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ be an eventually positive series. Let $S_m = \sum_{n=1}^{m} a_n$ denote its $m$-th partial sum.

Then there are only two possibilities:

1. If the sequence of partial sums $\{S_m\}{m=1}^{\infty}$ is bounded from above, then $\sum{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ converges.
2. If the sequence of partial sums $\{S_m\}{m=1}^{\infty}$ is not bounded from above, then $\sum{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ diverges to $\infty$.

**3.3.3 Integral Test**

Suppose that a function $f$ is continuous, positive, and decreasing on the infinite interval $[k, \infty)$ for some positive integer $k$. Then the following statements hold:

1. If $\displaystyle \int_{k}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx$ converges, then the infinite series $\displaystyle \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f(n)$ converges.
2. If $\displaystyle \int_{k}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx$ diverges, then the infinite series $\displaystyle \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f(n)$ diverges to $\infty$.

e.g. Let us consider the infinite series ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \frac{\ln (n)}{n}}$. We can express this series as ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^\infty f(n)}$, where ${\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{\ln (x)}{x}}$. f is clearly continuous and positive when $x≥1$. To check whether f is decreasing, we compute its derivative and get $f'(x)=\frac{1-\ln x}{x^{2}}$.

We note that $f'(x)=0$ if $x=e$, $f'(x) \gt 0$ if $0 \lt x \lt e$, and $f'(x) \lt 0$ if $x \gt e$. 

Hence we conclude that f is decreasing on the interval $[e,\infty )$. Since $e\approx 2.72$, 

we can use the Integral Test with $k=3$. (This is because f(x) will be decreasing on the subinterval $[3,\infty ) \subset [e,\infty )$.) It only remains to compute the corresponding improper integral. 

$\int _{3}^{\infty } \frac{\ln (x)}{x} d x=\left.\lim _{b \rightarrow \infty } \frac{(\ln (x))^{2}}{2}\right]_{3} ^{b}=\lim _{b \rightarrow \infty } \left(\frac{(\ln (b))^{2}}{2}- \frac{(\ln (3))^2}{2}\right)=\infty .$

Hence the series ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \frac{\ln (n)}{n}}$ diverges by the Integral Test.

**3.3.5 p-Series Test**

Let *p* be a real number. An infinite series of the form $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^p}$ is called a *p*-series.

1. If $p > 1$, then the corresponding *p*-series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^p}$ converges.
2. If $p ≤ 1$, then the corresponding *p*-series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^p}$ diverges.

**3.3.7 Integral Test Estimation Theorem**

Suppose that a function *f* is continuous, positive, and decreasing on the infinite interval $[k, ∞)$ for some positive integer $k$. Further suppose that $\int_k^{\infty} f(x) dx$ converges so that $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f(n) = S$ for some real number $S$ by the Integral Test. Let $S_m = \sum_{n=1}^{m} f(n)$denote the $m$-th partial sum. Then for any integer $m \geq k$, we have 

$\int_{m+1}^{\infty} f(x) dx \leq S - S_m \leq \int_m^{\infty} f(x) dx$ .

e.g. Find an upper bound on the error if we choose the partial sum $S_{10}$ to estimate ${\displaystyle S=\sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \frac{1}{n^{2}}}$. 

We first note that our infinite series is a convergent p-series with $p=2 \gt 1$. The error is $S-S_{10}$, which is the difference between the actual infinite sum and the chosen estimate $S_{10}$. If ${\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{1}{x^2}}$, then $f(x)$ satisfies the hypothesis of the Integral Test Estimation Theorem with $k=1$. By the second inequality of 3.5, we have 

$S-S_{10} \leq \int _{10}^{\infty } \frac{1}{x^{2}} d x =\lim _{b\to \infty }\left[ -\frac{1}{x}\right]_{10}^ b =\lim _{b\to \infty } \left(\frac{1}{10}-\frac{1}{b} \right) =\frac{1}{10}$

Hence the error in this estimate is at most ${\displaystyle \frac{1}{10}}$.

### 3.4 Comparison Test and Limit Comparison Test

**3.4.1 Comparison Test**

Let $k$ be a positive integer. Suppose that $\{a_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ and $\{b_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ are two sequences satisfying $0 \leq a_n \leq b_n$ for every $n \geq k$.

1. If $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n$ converges, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ converges too.
2. If $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ diverges, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n$ diverges too’

**3.4.3 Limit Comparison Test**

Let $k$ be a positive integer. Suppose that $\{a_n\}{n=1}^{\infty}$ *and $\{b_n\}{n=1}^{\infty}$* are two sequences satisfying $a_n \geq 0$ and $b_n > 0$ for every $n \geq k$. Let $L = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{a_n}{b_n}$. 
Then there are only four possibilities.

1. If $0 < L < \infty$, then either $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ and $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n$ both converge or both diverge.
2. If $L = 0$, then there is a positive integer $j$ such that $a_n < b_n$ for every $n \geq j$. Consequently, if $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n$converges, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ converges too. If $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ diverges, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n$ diverges too.
3. If $L = \infty$, then there is a positive integer $j$ such that $a_n > b_n$ for every $n \geq j$. Consequently, if $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ converges, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n$ converges too. If $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n$ diverges, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ diverges too.
4. If we know that the limit $L$ does not exist, then no conclusion can be drawn solely from that knowledge.

e.g. Consider the infinite series ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \frac{(\ln (n))^{3}}{\sqrt{n}}}$. First, we note that ‌$(ln⁡(n))3n≥0$ for every ‌$n≥1$, and hence our series is nonnegative. Let us compare this series against a strictly positive series ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}}$ using the Limit Comparison Test. We know that ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}}$  is a p-series with ${\displaystyle p=\frac{1}{2}\leq 1}$ , and hence it diverges by the p-Series Test. We can now compute that

$L=\lim _{n \rightarrow \infty } \frac{\left(\frac{(\ln (n))^{3}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)}{1 / \sqrt{n}}=\lim _{n \rightarrow \infty }(\ln (n))^{3}=\infty$. 

Since the “bottom” series ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}}$ diverges, the “top” series ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \frac{(\ln (n))^{3}}{\sqrt{n}}}$ must also diverge by part 3 of the Limit Comparison Test.

### 3.5 Alternating Series Test

**3.5.1 Alternating Series**

Let $\{a_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ be a strictly positive sequence, i.e., $a_n > 0$  for each integer $n \geq 1$. An alternating series is an infinite series that is in one of the following two forms:

1. $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{n-1} a_n = a_1 - a_2 + a_3 - a_4 + \cdots$, or
2.  $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^n a_n = -a_1 + a_2 - a_3 + a_4 - \cdots = -(a_1 - a_2 + a_3 - a_4 + \cdots) = - \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{n-1} a_n$

**3.5.3** 

An alternating series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{n-1} a_n$ converges (to a real number $S$) if and only if its negative $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^n a_n$ converges (to $-S$).

**3.5.4 Alternating Series Test**

Let $\{a_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ be a sequence satisfying the following two conditions.

1. $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = 0$. 
2. There is a positive integer $k$ such that $a_n \geq a_{n+1} > 0$ for every $n \geq k$. In other words, $\{a_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ is eventually decreasing (or eventually non-increasing) and eventually strictly positive.
Then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{n-1} a_n$ and $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^n a_n$ both converge.

e.g. (Alternating Harmonic Series)

Consider the infinite series ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}}$. It is alternating since it can be rewritten as ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } (-1)^{n-1}\, \frac{1}{n}}$. We can check that ${\displaystyle \lim _{n \rightarrow \infty } a_ n=\lim _{n \rightarrow \infty }\frac{1}{n}=0}$, and 

$a_ n=\frac{1}{n} \gt \frac{1}{n+1}=a_{n+1} \gt 0$

for every integer $n\geq 1$. Thus we can apply the Alternating Series Test (with $k=1$) and conclude that ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}}$ converges.

**3.5.7 Alternating Series Estimation Theorem**

Let $\{a_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ be a sequence that satisfies the hypotheses 1. and 2.  of the Alternating Series Test (Theorem 3.5.4) so that the corresponding alternating series $\sum{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{n-1} a_n$ and $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^n a_n$ converge to real numbers $S$ and $T$, respectively.

Let $S_m = \sum_{n=1}^{m} (-1)^{n-1} a_n$ denote the $m$-th partial sum of $S = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{n-1} a_n$. Let $T_m = \sum_{n=1}^{m} (-1)^n a_n$ denote the $m$-th partial sum of $T = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^n a_n$.

Suppose that $m \geq k$, where $k$ is the positive integer in the hypothesis 2. of the Alternating Series Test. Then we can conclude the following.

1. $|S - S_m| \leq a_{m+1}$ and $|T - T_m| \leq a_{m+1}$.
2. If $m$ is even, then $S_m$ is an underestimate of $S$ and $T_m$ is an overestimate of $T$.
3. If $m$ is odd, then $S_m$ is an overestimate of $S$ and $T_m$ is an underestimate of $T$.

e.g. How many terms are needed to estimate ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{10^{n}\, n !}}$ with an error that is at most 0.000005?

We can easily check that ${\displaystyle \lim _{n\to \infty } \frac{1}{10^{n}\, n !}=0}$, and that ${\displaystyle \frac{1}{10^{n}\, n !} \gt \frac{1}{10^{n+1}\, (n+1)!} \gt 0}$ for every $n\geq 1$. Thus we can apply the Alternating Series Test (with $k=1$) to conclude that our series converges to some real number $S$. By part (1) of the Alternating Series Estimation Theorem, the error in the estimate made by the partial sum $S_ m$ satisfies the inequality

$|\, \mathrm{error}\, |=\left|S-S_{m}\right| \leq a_{m+1}=\frac{1}{10^{m+1}(m+1) !}$.

Thus we want to find an integer m such that the inequality

$\frac{1}{10^{m+1}(m+1) !} \leq 0.000005$

is satisfied. Using a computer, we can check that 

$\frac{1}{10^{m+1}(m+1) !}=\left\{ \begin{array}{lll} {\displaystyle \frac{1}{10^{3}(3) !}} \approx 0.00017 & \mathrm{when} & m=2,\\[15pt] {\displaystyle \frac{1}{10^{4}(4) !}} \approx 0.000004 & \mathrm{when} & m=3. \end{array} \right.$

Thus $m=3$ satisfies our inequality and so three terms are enough.

### 3.6 Absolute and Conditional Convergence

**3.6.1 Absolute Convergence**

Given an infinite series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$, its absolutization is the infinite series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |a_n|$.

We say that the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ converges absolutely if its absolutization $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |a_n|$ converges.

**3.6.3 Absolute Convergence Test**

If $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |a_n|$ converges, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ converges.

**3.6.4 Conditional Convergence**

We say that an infinite series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ converges conditionally if it converges but its absolutization $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |a_n|$ diverges.

e.g. Determine whether each of the following series diverges, converges absolutely, or converges conditionally.

$\mathrm{(i)} \; \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \frac{(-1)^{n} 4^{n}}{3^{n}} \hspace{30pt} \mathrm{(ii)} \; \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \frac{e^{n}}{\pi ^{n}} \hspace{30pt} \mathrm{(iii)} \; \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \frac{(-1)^{n} \sqrt{n^{2}+n}}{n^{3 / 2}}$

1. Let us apply the Divergence Test (Corollary 3.2.6) first. Note that ${\displaystyle \lim _{n \rightarrow \infty } \frac{4^{n}}{3^{n}}=\lim _{n \rightarrow \infty } \left(\frac{4}{3}\right)^ n}=\infty$. Hence, if we add alternating signs, then ${\displaystyle \lim _{n \rightarrow \infty } \frac{(-1)^{n} 4^{n}}{3^{n}}}$ does not exist. By the Divergence Test, our series diverges.
2. Note that ${\displaystyle \lim _{n \rightarrow \infty } \frac{e^{n}}{\pi ^{n}}=\lim _{n \rightarrow \infty } \left(\frac{e}{\pi }\right)^ n=0}$, since ${\displaystyle 0 \lt \frac{e}{\pi } \lt 1}$. Thus the Divergence Test fails.

We also note that this series only has positive terms. Thus absolute convergence is equivalent to ordinary convergence for this series. In fact, our series is a geometric series with ratio ${\displaystyle \frac{e}{\pi }}$. Since ${\displaystyle \left|\frac{e}{\pi }\right|=\frac{e}{\pi } \lt 1}$, our series converges absolutely by the Geometric Series Test.

1. Let us apply the Divergence Test first. Dividing the numerator and the denominator by n, we conclude that

$\lim _{n\to \infty }\frac{\sqrt{n^{2}+n}}{n^{3 / 2}} =\lim _{n\to \infty }\frac{\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{n}}}{n^{1 / 2}} =0$. 

Since we have $-\frac{\sqrt{n^{2}+n}}{n^{3 / 2}} \leq \frac{(-1)^{n} \sqrt{n^{2}+n}}{n^{3 / 2}} \leq \frac{\sqrt{n^{2}+n}}{n^{3 / 2}}$

for every integer $n\geq 1$, the Squeeze Theorem implies that ${\displaystyle \lim _{n\to \infty } \frac{(-1)^{n} \sqrt{n^{2}+n}}{n^{3 / 2}}=0}$. Thus the Divergence Test fails.

Next, let us check for absolute convergence. The absolutization of our series is $\sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \left| \frac{(-1)^{n} \sqrt{n^{2}+n}}{n^{3 / 2}}\right|=\sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \frac{\sqrt{n^{2}+n}}{n^{3 / 2}}$, 

which is clearly a strictly positive series. Asymptotically, as $n\to \infty$, the general term ${\displaystyle \frac{\sqrt{n^{2}+n}}{n^{3 / 2}}}$ is similar to ${\displaystyle \frac{\sqrt{n^{2}}}{n^{3 / 2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}}$ after we ignore the lower order terms. Thus it makes sense to compare our series against the strictly positive series ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}}$ using the Limit Comparison Test. We note that ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}}$ diverges by the p-Series Test because it is a p-series with ${\displaystyle p=\frac{1}{2}\leq 1}$. We can now compute that

$L=\lim _{n \rightarrow \infty } \frac{\left(\frac{\sqrt{n^{2}+n}}{n^{3 / 2}}\right)}{1 / \sqrt{n}}=\lim _{n \rightarrow \infty } \frac{\sqrt{n^{2}+n}}{n}=\lim _{n \rightarrow \infty } \sqrt{1+\frac{1}{n}}=1.$

Since $0 \lt L=1 \lt \infty$, we conclude that ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \frac{\sqrt{n^{2}+n}}{n^{3 / 2}}}$ must diverge too by part (i) of the Limit Comparison Test. Hence our series does not converge absolutely.

Finally, let us check for conditional convergence using the Alternating Series Test. We already saw that ${\displaystyle \lim _{n \rightarrow \infty } \frac{\sqrt{n^{2}+n}}{n^{3 / 2}}=0}$, and thus hypothesis (i) of the Alternating Series Test is satisfied. Since ${\displaystyle \frac{\sqrt{n^{2}+n}}{n^{3 / 2}}}$ is strictly positive for every integer $n≥1$, we only need to check that this sequence is eventually decreasing (or eventually non-increasing) to fulfill hypothesis (ii) of the Alternating Series Test. We can compute that

$\begin{aligned} \frac{\sqrt{n^{2}+n}}{n^{3 / 2}} - \frac{\sqrt{(n+1)^{2}+(n+1)}}{(n+1)^{3 / 2}} & = \frac{\sqrt{n}\cdot \sqrt{n+1}}{n\sqrt{n}} -\frac{\sqrt{n+1}\cdot \sqrt{(n+1)+1}}{(n+1)\sqrt{n+1}}\\[2pt]& = \frac{\sqrt{n+1}}{n} -\frac{\sqrt{(n+1)+1}}{n+1} =\frac{\sqrt{n+1}}{n} -\frac{\sqrt{n+2}}{n+1}\\[2pt]& =\frac{(n+1)\sqrt{n+1}-n\sqrt{n+2}}{n(n+1)}\\[2pt]& =\frac{(n+1)^2(n+1)-n^2(n+2)}{n(n+1)\Big((n+1)\sqrt{n+1}+n\sqrt{n+2}\Big)}\, \end{aligned}$

The last denominator is clearly strictly positive. The last numerator is equal to 

$(n+1)^3-n^3-2n^2=n^2+3n+1$, 

which is strictly positive for every integer $n≥1$. It follows that

$\frac{\sqrt{n^{2}+n}}{n^{3 / 2}} - \frac{\sqrt{(n+1)^{2}+(n+1)}}{(n+1)^{3 / 2}} \; \gt \; 0$

for every integer $n\geq 1$. Hence the sequence ${\displaystyle \left\{ \frac{\sqrt{n^{2}+n}}{n^{3 / 2}}\right\} _{n=1}^\infty }$ is strictly decreasing and hypothesis (ii) of the Alternating Series Test is satisfied. By the Alternating Series Test, we conclude that ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \frac{(-1)^{n} \sqrt{n^{2}+n}}{n^{3 / 2}}}$ converges. Since the absolutization of our series does not converge, our series converges conditionally.

### 3.7 Ratio Test and Root Test

**3.7.1 Ratio Test** 

Let $k$ be a positive integer. Suppose that $\{a_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ is a sequence satisfying $a_n \neq 0$ for every $n \geq k$.

Let $L = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left| \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} \right|$

Further, suppose that either $L \in \mathbb{R}$ or $L = \infty$.

1. If $L < 1$, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ converges absolutely.
2. If $L > 1$ or if $L = \infty$, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ diverges.
3. If we know that $L = 1$, then no conclusion can be drawn solely from that knowledge.

Remark: If ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^\infty a_ n}$ is a conditionally convergent series that satisfies the hypothesis of the Ratio Test, then we must have $L=1$. 

e.g. Consider the infinite series ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \frac{(-1)^{n}\, 2^{n}}{n\, 3^{n}}}$. We can rewrite our series as ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \frac{1}{n}\left(-\frac{2}{3}\right)^ n}$. 

Every term of our series is nonzero, and we can compute that

$L=\lim _{n \rightarrow \infty }\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_{n}}\right|=\lim _{n \rightarrow \infty }\left| \frac{1}{n+1}\left(-\frac{2}{3}\right)^{n+1}\! \cdot n\left(-\frac{3}{2}\right)^{n}\right|=\lim _{n \rightarrow \infty } \frac{n}{n+1} \cdot \frac{2}{3} =\frac{2}{3} \lt 1$. 

By part (i) of the Ratio Test, our series ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty } \frac{(-1)^{n}\, 2^{n}}{n\, 3^{n}}}$ converges absolutely.

**3.7.5 Root Test**

Let $L = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sqrt[n]{|a_n|}$, and suppose that $L \in \mathbb{R}$ or $L = \infty$.

1. If $L < 1$, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ converges absolutely.
2. If $L > 1$ or if $L = \infty$, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n$ diverges.
3. If we know that $L = 1$, then no conclusion can be drawn solely from that knowledge.

e.g. Let us apply the Root Test to the infinite series ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty }\left(\frac{n+1}{3 n+7}\right)^{n}}$. 

We compute that $L=\lim _{n \rightarrow \infty }\left(\, \left|\left(\frac{n+1}{3 n+7}\right)^{n}\right|\, \right)^{1 / n}=\lim _{n \rightarrow \infty } \frac{n+1}{3 n+7}=\frac{1}{3} \lt 1$  

By part (i) of the Root Test, our series ${\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty }\left(\frac{n+1}{3 n+7}\right)^{n}}$ converges absolutely.
